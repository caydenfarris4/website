I spent the last week in Denmark, standing in the churches where my ancestors worshiped.

I walked the cemeteries where we believe they are buried. I went through museums built around the way of life they called their own - the tools they used, the trades they kept, the rhythm of a world that assumed you would become what your family had always been.

I went there to see where I came from.

I left thinking about mentorship.

---

## If your father was a carpenter

Here is how their world worked. If your father was a carpenter, you became a carpenter. You learned at his bench, with his tools, with his hands correcting yours until the work was right. His father had taught him the same way. Nobody called it mentorship. Nobody called it anything. It was simply how a life got built.

We look back at that world and see a ceiling. No options. No reinvention. Your path handed to you before you could speak.

But standing in those churches, I kept seeing something else. Nobody in that world believed they were supposed to figure life out alone. Learning from someone who had already walked the road was not a confession of weakness. It was the entire system. Every skill in the village was the accumulated work of generations, passed hand to hand, each one adding to what they had received before handing it on.

They inherited the path. We inherited the pride.

---

## The trail that looks like a monster

On Chichagof Island in Southeast Alaska, hikers keep finding something on the deep mountain ridges that stops them cold. Rows of massive footprints, pressed deep into the moss, spaced with an eerie regularity, worn into the earth like something enormous has been pacing that ridge for a very long time.

People have famously mistaken them for Sasquatch tracks.

The truth is better than the legend. They are called grandfather trails, and they are made by brown bears - generations of them. Each bear places its paws in the exact footprints of the bears that came before it. Step into step, year after year, until the prints are stamped inches into the ground. A bear walking a grandfather trail is walking in the footsteps of ancestors it never met.

Why do they do it? Because the trail works. The ridge line holds. The route is proven. A young bear that follows the footprints does not get lost.

Most people hear that and think it is a story about conformity. I think it is a story about navigation. The bear is not copying because it lacks imagination. It is stepping into footprints that hold generations of tested knowledge about where the ground is solid.

> "A deep footprint is not a limitation. It is a message from someone who made it through."

---

## The myth of the self-made man

We have built a culture that does the exact opposite.

We celebrate the self-made man. We tell the story of the person who owed nothing to nobody, who figured it out alone, who never needed a hand. And because that is the story we honor, asking for guidance starts to feel like an admission. People push away help - not because they do not need it, but because accepting it would ruin the story they are trying to tell about themselves.

John Maxwell says one of the greatest values of mentors is the ability to see ahead what others cannot see, and to help them navigate a course to their destination.

Most people read that as a compliment to mentors. I read it as a diagnosis of the rest of us. If a mentor's value is seeing ahead on the trail, then refusing one is choosing to walk the ridge blind - in the fog, at night, proudly.

Nobody is self-made. There are only people who know whose footprints they stepped in and people who pretend the trail was empty when they got there.

---

## The footsteps are not the trade

Here is where my ancestors' world and mine split apart.

My dad is a lawyer. I am a product manager. By the logic of the old villages, there is no trail here to follow - his trade and mine barely share a vocabulary. You could look at my parents and say there is no way they could mentor my career.

I see it differently.

They do not mentor my career. They mentor me. And the principles of success are the same across every trade that has ever existed. My parents walk ahead of me in resilience. In dedication. In love. In faith. Every bit of success I have been able to see - the records in support, the product role at twenty-two, the company I founded - traces back to footprints they pressed into the ground long before I understood what I was stepping into.

The trade is the surface. The principles are the trail. My ancestors followed both because in their world the two happened to overlap. In ours they usually do not - and that has confused us into thinking the trail is gone.

It is not gone. It just is not the career anymore. It never really was.

## Step in

So here is my invitation, and it comes in two parts.

Find a mentor. Not necessarily someone who physically walked your path - someone whose principles have been tested, someone who can see ahead of you on the ridge, someone who can build you up in ways you will not fully understand until years later. They are closer than you think, and most of them are waiting to be asked.

And then be one. Somebody is behind you on the trail right now, whether you have noticed them or not. You do not have to be all-knowing to pass on what you know. You do not have to have arrived. You just have to be one step further up the trail than the person following you, and honest about what the ground was like when you crossed it.
